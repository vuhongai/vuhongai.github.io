*The foundation of my work: discovering why muscle cells fail in muscular dystrophy — and how those discoveries reveal new, treatable weak points.*

---

## The Problem

**Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD)** is a severe genetic disease that affects about one in 5,000 boys. A single faulty gene deprives muscle of a protein called dystrophin, which normally acts like a shock absorber protecting muscle fibers as they contract. Without it, muscle slowly breaks down, and patients lose the ability to walk, then breathe.

For decades, the field focused almost entirely on this mechanical damage. But that story was incomplete: dystrophic muscle also shows deep problems in its **metabolism** — the chemistry that keeps cells alive and powered. When I began my doctoral research, why this happened, and whether it could be treated, was largely unknown. I set out to answer that question, using large biological datasets to find patterns that pointed to the real culprits.

---

## What We Discovered

My work uncovered **two hidden, treatable problems** in dystrophic muscle — both missed by looking at dystrophin alone.

<div class="pipeline-steps" data-aos="fade-up">
  <div class="pipeline-step">
    <div class="step-num">1</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>The cell's power plants are switched off.</strong> I found that a coordinated group of tiny regulatory molecules becomes overactive in DMD and dials down the muscle's <em>mitochondria</em> — the power plants that generate energy. This reframed the energy crisis in DMD not as a side effect of damage, but as an actively controlled program that could, in principle, be switched back on. It also explained part of <em>why steroids</em> — the standard DMD treatment — help.</div>
  </div>
  <div class="pipeline-step">
    <div class="step-num">2</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Cholesterol builds up and clogs the recycling system.</strong> Using a new way of reading these same molecular signals, I traced a second problem to <em>cholesterol</em>. Dystrophic muscle accumulates excess cholesterol — confirmed in patient samples — which damages the cell's waste-recycling compartments (lysosomes). Lowering cholesterol with a common statin drug eased the disease in mice, identifying an existing, inexpensive class of drugs as a potential add-on therapy.</div>
  </div>
</div>

These findings, published across journals including *Journal of Cachexia, Sarcopenia and Muscle*, *Life Science Alliance*, *Scientific Reports*, and *Science Advances*, established metabolism as an active driver of muscular dystrophy — and a promising target for treatment alongside gene therapy.

---

## Why It Still Matters

This research shaped everything I do now. It taught me that **finding a treatable weak point is only half the battle** — you also need a way to reach it safely inside the body. That realization is exactly what pushed me toward engineering better delivery vehicles and antibody-clearing proteins: the technologies needed to *act* on the biology we uncover.

Understanding disease and building the tools to treat it are, for me, two halves of the same mission.
